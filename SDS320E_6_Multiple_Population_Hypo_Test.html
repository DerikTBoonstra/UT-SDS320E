Hypothesis Testing for Two Population Means: Paired & Independent T

Hypothesis Testing for Two Pop Means

  • We may be interested in determining if there is a difference between two means
    • i.e. is \(\mu_{1} = \mu_{2}\)? \(\mu_{1}\) & \(\mu_{2}\) is mean for population 1 and 2, respectively
  • To perform a hypothesis test for \(\mu_{1}\) and \(\mu_{2}\), we must determine:
    • Are the two populations independent or dependent of one another?

  • Independent Populations: populations are independent of one another if they have no influence on, and are completely unrelated to, other populations i.e. the outcomes from populations are not dependent on one another

  • Dependent Populations: populations are dependent on one another if the outcomes from one population influence, or are related to, the outcomes from other populations

    • Why does dependence matter?

Why Dependence Matters

Example

Suppose one is interested in determining if there is a difference between \(\mu\) = the mean SAT scores for the genders Male and Female. For \(H_{0}\), we assume that there is no difference in SAT scores between genders. That is,

\[H_{0}: \mu_{Female} = \mu_{Male}\]

Previously, we said p-value \(= 0.02 < \alpha = 0.05\). Thus, there is sufficient evidence that there is a significant difference between \(\mu_{Female}\) and \(\mu_{Male}\). However, are there other factors aside from gender that can cause a difference in SAT scores?

Dependent populations can eliminate confounding factors

    • pre and post treatment from the same subject
    • “pair” subjects based upon similarities most common
    • genetics: twins, litters, ect.

Hypothesis Test for Two Dependent Means

Paired t-Test

Let \(X_{1}\) and \(X_{2}\) represent populations 1 and 2, respectively, and, let \(X_{d} = X_{1} - X_{2}\) and \(\mu_{d} = \mu_{1} - \mu_{2}\). Then, o conduct a hypothesis test for two dependent population means, we will use the Paired t-Test:

Null and Alternative Hypothesis

\(H_{0}: \mu_{d} = value\) typically \(value = 0\), i.e. \(\mu_{1} = \mu_{2}\)

\(H_{a}: \mu_{d} < value\), \(> value\), or \(\neq value\)

Test-Statistic and Distribution

\(t\) \(= \frac{\bar{x}_{d} - \mu_{d}}{s_{d}/\sqrt{n}}\) \(\sim t(df = n - 1)\)
again \(\mu_{d} = 0\) is the common case

Assumption for Paired t-Test: Normality of the \(X_{d}\) (check with QQ-Plot of the differenced data)

  • To do a paired t-Test in \(\texttt{R}\), we will just the t.test() function on the differenced data

Paired t-Test in R

  • To conduct a paired t-test in R, we can create the differenced data \(X_{d} = X_{1} - X_{2}\) and then use the t.test() function on it. The arguments of interest are:
    • x: the numeric vector of the differenced data
    • mu: the hypothesized value of \(\mu_{d}\) in \(H_{0}\) (default is 0)
    • alternative: the form of \(H_{a}\), either "less", "greater", or "two.sided" (default is "two.sided")
  • Or, we can keep our variables separate and use t.test(x, y, paired = TRUE)

Hypothesis Test for Two Independent Means

Pooled t-Test

Let \(X_{1}\) and \(X_{2}\) represent populations 1 and 2, respectively, with means \(\mu_{1}\) and \(\mu_{2}\). Then, to conduct a hypothesis test for two independent population means, we will use the Pooled t-Test:

Null and Alternative Hypothesis

\(H_{0}: \mu_{1} - \mu_{2} = value\) typically \(value = 0\), i.e. \(\mu_{1} = \mu_{2}\)

\(H_{a}: \mu_{1} - \mu_{2} < value\), \(> value\), or \(\neq value\)

Test-Statistic and Distribution

\(t\) \(= \frac{(\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}) - (\mu_{1} - \mu_{2})}{\sqrt{\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{n_{1}} + \frac{s_{2}^{2}}{n_{2}}}}\) \(\sim t(df = n_{1} + n_{2} - 2)\)
again \(\mu_{1} - \mu_{2} = 0\) is the common case

Assumptions for Pooled t-Test

  1. Normality of both \(X_{1}\) and \(X_{2}\) (check with a QQ-Plot for each sample)
  2. Equal Variances (i.e. assumes \(\sigma_{1} = \sigma_{2}\). Check with boxplots/densities/histograms)
  • Note: If the equal variance assumption is not reasonable, then we could perform Welch’s Unpooled t-Test (less powerful than pooled t-Test)

Pooled t-Test in R

  • To conduct a pooled t-test in R, we can keep the two samples separate and use the t.test() function with var.equal = TRUE. The arguments of interest are:
    • x, y: the numeric vectors of the data for population 1 and population 2, respectively
    • mu: the hypothesized value of \(\mu_{1} - \mu_{2}\) in \(H_{0}\) (default is 0)
    • alternative: the form of \(H_{a}\), either "less", "greater", or "two.sided" (default is "two.sided")
    • var.equal: set to TRUE for the pooled test (default is FALSE, which gives the unpooled test)
  • Or, if the data is in one column with a second column of group labels, we can use the formula version t.test(y ~ group, data = data, var.equal = TRUE)

Test for Multiple Populations Cont.

  • For testing one and two population means, we often use the t-test
    • For testing more than two population means, we need a different approach
  • Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is a statistical method used to compare the means of three or more groups to determine whether there is a statistically significant difference among them.
  • ANOVA examines how the dependent/response variable changes with the levels of the factor(s)
    • A factor is a qualitative variable that represents conditions, groups, or categories Ex: Factor is hair color with levels of brown, black, blonde, red, gray
    • The dependent/response variable is the quantitative outcome being measured and compared across the different groups.
    • A one-factor ANOVA examines how a dependent/response variable changes over the levels of a single factor

One-Factor ANOVA

One-Factor ANOVA

Let \(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{K}\) represent K different populations and \(\mu_k, k = 1, \ldots, K\) are the population means for a specific variable measured across K different populations. Then, to conduct a hypothesis test to determine whether the mean is different or the same across the K populations, we will use ANOVA:

Null and Alternative Hypothesis

\(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \ldots = \mu_K\) i.e. the means are all the same

\(H_{a}: \text{At least one mean } \mu \text{ is different.}\)

Test-Statistic and Distribution

\(F = \frac{\sum_{k = 1}^{K}n_{k}(\bar{X}_{k} - \bar{X})^{2}/(K - 1)}{\sum_{k = 1}^{K}\sum_{i = 1}^{n_{k}}(X_{i} - \bar{X}_{k})^{2}/(n - K)}\) \(= \frac{MSB}{MSW}\) \(\sim F(df_{1} = K - 1, df_{2} = n - K)\)
F is another distribution like \(t\), normal, ect.

Assumptions for ANOVA

  1. Normality of all populations Check w/ QQ plots
  2. Equal Variance Check with boxplots

Multiple Comparisons for ANOVA

Reminder

The hypotheses for ANOVA are \[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3\\ H_a&: \text{At least one mean } \mu \text{ is different,} \end{align*}\]

  • Thus, if p-value \(< \alpha\), we reject \(H_{0}\) and conclude we have sufficient evidence that at least one mean is different
    • So, the question becomes which one(s) is/are different from one another?
  • The goal of multiple comparison analysis is to determine which mean(s) is different from the rest sometimes all of the means are different

Multiple Comparisons for ANOVA Cont.

  • We could answer this by performing pairwise hypothesis tests i.e. test each possible pair of groups

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_2 \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2 \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_3 \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_3 \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_2 = \mu_3 \\ H_a&: \mu_2 \neq \mu_3 \end{align*}\]

  • Intuitively this method is valid, however, statistically it creates a problem

Issue

When we run many hypothesis tests, we increase the chance of finding a “significant” difference just by “luck”. Reminder: In hypothesis testing, we never know if our conclusion is correct

  • Now, imagine if we have 100 means to compare…

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_2 \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2 \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_3 \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_3 \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_3 \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_3 \quad \ldots \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_{100} \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_{100} \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_2 = \mu_3 \\ H_a&: \mu_2 \neq \mu_3 \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_2 = \mu_4 \\ H_a&: \mu_2 \neq \mu_4 \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_2 = \mu_5 \\ H_a&: \mu_2 \neq \mu_5 \quad \ldots \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_2 = \mu_{100} \\ H_a&: \mu_2 \neq \mu_{100} \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_1 = \mu_2 \\ H_a&: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2 \end{align*}\]

\(\ldots\)

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_{97} = \mu_{98} \\ H_a&: \mu_{97} \neq \mu_{98} \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_{97} = \mu_{99} \\ H_a&: \mu_{97} \neq \mu_{99} \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} H_0&: \mu_{97} = \mu_{100} \\ H_a&: \mu_{97} \neq \mu_{100} \end{align*}\]

  • That leaves a lot of chance for error, which we call the family-wise error rate (FWER)

\(FWER = P(\text{at least one error}) = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^{\text{# of test}}\)
This is a lot bigger than our original \(\alpha\)!

Tukey’s Multiple Comparison Adjustment

  • We need a way to adjust for this inflation of error before drawing conclusions

Tukey’s Multiple Comparison

After rejecting \(H_{0}\) in ANOVA, we use Tukey’s Multiple Comparison to determine which pairs of means are different, while keeping the family-wise error rate at \(\alpha\):

Null and Alternative Hypothesis

\(H_0: \mu_i = \mu_j\) for every pair of groups \(i \neq j\)

\(H_{a}: \mu_i \neq \mu_j\)

Conclusions

  • If the adjusted p-value \(< \alpha\), we conclude the two means are different
  • Or, if the interval for \(\mu_i - \mu_j\) does not contain 0, we conclude the two means are different
  • To perform Tukey’s Multiple Comparison in R, we use TukeyHSD() with aov()
aov_results <- aov(response ~ factor, data = my_data)
TukeyHSD(aov_results)

Hypothesis Testing for Categorical Data: \(\chi^{2}\) GOF

Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test

Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test

Let a categorical variable have K categories, and let \(p_k, k = 1, \ldots, K\) be the population proportions of each category. Then, to conduct a hypothesis test to determine whether the proportions match a hypothesized distribution, we will use the Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test:

Null and Alternative Hypothesis

\(H_0: p_1 = p_{1,0},\ p_2 = p_{2,0},\ \ldots,\ p_K = p_{K,0}\) sometimes \(p_{1} = p_{2} = \ldots = p_{K} = 1/K\)

\(H_{a}: \text{At least one proportion } p_k \text{ is different from its hypothesized value.}\)

Test-Statistic and Distribution

\(\chi^{2} = \sum_{k = 1}^{K}\frac{(O_{k} - E_{k})^{2}}{E_{k}}\) \(\stackrel{\cdot}{\sim} \chi^{2}(df = K - 1)\)
\(O_{k}\) = observed count, \(E_{k} = n \cdot p_{k,0}\) = expected count if \(H_{0}\) is true

Assumptions for Chi-Square Goodness of Fit

  1. “Sufficiently” large sample size: All expected counts are at least 5 \(E_{k} \geq 5\)

Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test in R

  • To conduct a chi-square goodness of fit test in R, we can use the chisq.test() function on the observed counts. The arguments of interest are:
    • x: the numeric vector of the observed counts \(O_{k}\) for each category
    • p: the vector of hypothesized proportions \(p_{k,0}\) in \(H_{0}\), in the same order as x (default is equal proportions)
  • Or, if we have the raw data, we can first use table() to get the counts and then use chisq.test(table(data), p = p)
  • To check the assumption that all expected counts are at least 5, we can use $expected

Example

The latest Emerson College poll for the Texas Senate race polled 947 decided voters that were likely to vote. Of the 947, 476 said they would vote for James Talarico (D), 458 for Ken Paxton (R), and 13 for Ted Brown (L). We want to make a conclusion at the 5% level of significance on whether there is a significant difference in the proportion of likely voters who support each of the three candidates.

  1. What test should we perform?

\(\chi^{2}\) GOF test because we are comparing multiple categories.

  1. What are the null and alternative hypothesis?

\(H_0: p_{D} = p_{R} = p_{L} = 1/3\)

\(H_a: \text{At least one } p_{k} \text{ is different than hypothesized}\)

  1. Are the assumptions for the Test met?

The assumption is \(E_{k} = n \cdot p_{k} \geq 5\). Here, \(E_{k} = 947 \cdot (1/3) > 5\) for all \(k\).

4. Make a conclusion for the test.

The p-value < 0.001 < \(\alpha = 0.05\). Therefore, we reject \(H_{0}\) and have sufficient evidence to conclude that at least one proportion is significantly different than assumed.

  1. From the poll, Talarico “had” 476 votes \(>\) Ken Paxton’s 458 votes. Does our conclusion support that Talarico will win? We don’t know! It only supports that at least one candidate’s proportion of votes is different than 1/3.

Bonferroni Confidence Intervals

  • Since we rejected \(H_{0}\), the natural question is: which proportion(s) are different from the hypothesized value?
    • We can answer this by estimating each proportion with a confidence interval
  • However, just like multiple hypothesis tests, multiple confidence intervals inflate our overall \(\alpha\)

Bonferroni Confidence Intervals

To build \(K\) confidence intervals while keeping the overall level of significance at \(\alpha\), we use the Bonferroni adjustment:

We will split \(\alpha\) across the \(K\) different confidence intervals (i.e. each interval gets \(\alpha/K\) instead of \(\alpha\)) and construct \(K\) intervals at \((1 - \alpha/K) \times 100\%\) level of confidence.

Bonferroni CI in \(\texttt{R}\): Copy, paste, and save this function to your environment in \(\texttt{R}\).

bonferroni_ci <- function(observed, alpha = 0.05) {
  K <- length(observed)
  n <- sum(observed)
  lapply(observed, function(x) {
    prop.test(x, n, conf.level = 1 - alpha / K, correct = FALSE)$conf.int
  })
}
  • observed: the numeric vector of the observed counts for each category
  • alpha: the overall (family-wise) level of significance (default is 0.05)

Example

The latest Emerson College poll for the Texas Senate race polled 947 decided voters that were likely to vote. Of the 947, 476 said they would vote for James Talarico (D), 458 for Ken Paxton (R), and 13 for Ted Brown (L). We want to make a conclusion at the 5% level of significance on whether there is a significant difference in the proportion of likely voters who support each of the three candidates.

  1. Since we rejected \(H_{0}\), which candidate(s) have a proportion different from assumed?

  • CI for Talarico: (46.38%, 54.14%). Because the CI does not contain 1/3, we conclude the proportion of votes is different (specifically, larger than) 1/3.

  • CI for Paxton: (44.50%, 52.25%). Proportion of votes is significantly larger than 1/3.

  • CI for Brown: (0.72%, 2.61%) Proportion of votes is significantly less than 1/3.

Note: This tells us which proportions are different than assumed. But, we can not make a conclusion on whether Talarico or Paxton will win.

Bonferroni Confidence Intervals: Multiple Comparisons

  • Since we rejected \(H_0\), we know that the candidates do not have equal support.
    • But can we conclude that one candidate is more likely to win?
  • To answer this, we compare the population proportions for each pair of candidates.
    • We construct bonferroni confidence intervals for the differences between proportions, \(p_i-p_j\).
